# Reveal and foreshadowing techniques (twist_delivery)

Study only. No story, lore, ending or twist is chosen or proposed here. Research date: 2026-11-01.

Method caveats:
- Pages were read through the fetch tool, which returns an extract with short quotes. Quotes below are as the tool reported them. Spot-check any quote before it goes into the paper.
- "The page does not mention X" means the extract did not mention it. It may be incomplete (the extract missed Brothers' swim mechanic and TLOU2's named enemies on pages where other sources confirm them).
- About 60 tool calls were used, in parallel batches, because the assignment named about 20 works. No browser tools were used.

## 1. Mundane task, hidden harm: how do these works use UI, euphemism, distance and abstraction?

### Takeaway
The harm stays off-screen or inside a bland label while the interface stays bureaucratic: stamps, bins, datachunks, grey delayed feeds, flat radio voices. The reveal lands when a label is decoded, a delay ends, or the camera finally shows the aftermath.

### Cited Findings
Severance (TV)
- Helly's job is to "sort cryptic numbers into categories" (S1E2). Lumon names things softly: "wellness check" (S1E2), "Music Dance Experience" (S1E7), "Outdoor Retreat and Team-Building Occurrence" (S2E4). The break room is "used for mentally wearing down disobedient employees." Source: [Wikipedia: Severance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Severance_(TV_series))
- Refiners finish a "room" by sorting number clusters into digital buckets. The clusters provoke feelings because they represent the Four Tempers (Woe, Frolic, Malice, Dread). The work is described as "mysterious and important." Source: [Nerdist](https://nerdist.com/article/severance-finally-explained-macrodata-refinement-what-the-numbers-mean/)
- The same article says the numbers are "a doorway into the mind" of a subject, and its closing questions are openly speculative ("We have no idea, just theories"). Source: [Nerdist](https://nerdist.com/article/severance-finally-explained-macrodata-refinement-what-the-numbers-mean/)
- Set design as theme: the MDR room has a low ceiling "to create the feeling of being trapped," lighting is "cool, fluorescent overhead lighting," frames are often "frame within frame shots," and the computers "lacked an escape key." Source: [Wikipedia: Severance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Severance_(TV_series))
- Clue planted early, paid late: Irving's paintings of "a dark corridor" (S1E8) match a location later called the "Exports Hall" (S2E3). Source: [Wikipedia: Severance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Severance_(TV_series))
- Elevators carry the switch between selves in cited scenes (Mark transitions to his outie in the testing-floor elevator in the S2 finale; Gemma reverts to Ms. Casey when she tries to escape by elevator). The page does not describe the routine trigger. Source: [Wikipedia: Severance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Severance_(TV_series))
- Creator Dan Erickson on the origin: he wanted to "skip the eight hours of the workday, to disassociate and just get it over with." Source: [Wikipedia: Severance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Severance_(TV_series))

Papers, Please
- The loop: review passports and documents "against an ever-growing list of rules," stamp to accept or deny, on a clock representing a full day shift. Source: [Wikipedia: Papers, Please](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Papers,_Please)
- Moral weight arrives as numbers: pay per processed entrant plus bribes minus citation penalties, and a daily budget for "rent, food, heat and other necessities for their family." Debt ends the game. Source: [Wikipedia: Papers, Please](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Papers,_Please)
- Lucas Pope kept the end-of-day family status screen simple "to avoid swaying players' feelings too much," aimed for a non-judgmental narrative, called the work "other people simulators," and removed obvious political markers such as "comrade" from translations. Source: [Wikipedia: Papers, Please](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Papers,_Please)
- Pope on the premise: inspectors have "a specific thing they're doing and they're just doing it over and over again." Source: [Wikipedia: Papers, Please](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Papers,_Please)

Orwell: Keeping an Eye on You
- The player "monitors surveillance sources to find national security threats," reads correspondence and connects people. Uploading is the key decision: the player is limited to 20 datachunks and "followup on uploaded chunks is mandatory." Source: [Wikipedia: Orwell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orwell_(video_game))
- The system is "codenamed Orwell" and was created under a Safety Bill that expanded spying "in the name of national security." Outcomes include arrests and a leak to a newspaper. Source: [Wikipedia: Orwell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orwell_(video_game))
- Osmotic's own account: the concept started as "making a game that was set entirely on the desktop of a computer." They said "We especially didn't want to make it easy for them to decide which data to pass on to the government" and "We actually wanted the player to feel bad about certain decisions." Source: [Game Developer interview](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/road-to-the-igf-osmotic-studios-i-orwell-i-)

Beholder
- The player is "a government-installed landlord" who bugs apartments, searches belongings, builds profiles, and must report suspects. Source: [Wikipedia: Beholder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beholder_(video_game))
- The 2017 DLC "Blissful Sleep" has a bill exposing citizens aged 85 to euthanasia. The player can submit to a "Blissful sleep." The protagonist's age is wrongly recorded as 85 through a bureaucratic error. Source: [Wikipedia: Beholder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beholder_(video_game))
- The team focused on Papers, Please, This War of Mine, and the works of Orwell and Huxley while developing it. Source: [Wikipedia: Beholder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beholder_(video_game))

Unmanned (Molleindustria, 2012)
- Jorge Albor (Game Developer, September 2012): the player follows a drone pilot, Kirk, over two days, alternating home and work. A dream opens the game with Kirk turning into a drone just before waking. Most play runs two activities at once, a mission on one screen and conversation or domestic life on the other. Source: [Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/unmanned-and-the-rhetoric-of-division)
- Abstraction and distance in the same essay: a "fuzzy and grey video feed," a tracking system that drifts, "a noticeable delay" between a missile launch and its explosion, and medals with absurd names such as "Excellence Earned in Shaving." Albor reads the boredom as intentional and the procedure as "sanitized murder." Source: [Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/unmanned-and-the-rhetoric-of-division)
- Eron Rauch (Video Games for the Arts): "a series of mini-games" through a day (grooming regulations, commute, calling a spouse, remote combat, playing military shooters with a child), alternating "the banal and the tragic," "darkly ironic" but "nuanced." Source: [Video Games for the Arts](https://www.videogamesforthearts.com/articles/unmanned)
- Festival listing text: "the conflict is internal" and "the only blood you'll shed is from shaving cuts"; it asks whether this way of waging war causes its own collateral damage. Credit: "Dev. Molleindustria." Source: [Fantastic Arcade](https://fantasticarcade.com/games/2012/unmanned)
- Molleindustria's self-description, as quoted by Albor: "homeopathic remedies to the idiocy of mainstream entertainment." Source: [Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/unmanned-and-the-rhetoric-of-division)

Call of Duty 4: "Death From Above" (AC-130)
- Eighth mission. The player is a gunner aboard an AC-130 giving close air support to Captain Price's SAS unit. A navigator says "This is going to be one helluva highlight reel!" (Community wiki; the page does not describe the interface.) Source: [Call of Duty Wiki](https://callofduty.fandom.com/wiki/Death_From_Above)
- Hanno Terbuyken (evangelisch.de, 7 April 2010): the gunship pilot's tone is flat and detached, with lines such as "Good kill, good kill." He says the crew's commentary in the leaked "Collateral Murder" video sounds almost identical to the game's dialogue, and that "the real scandal is not the shooting itself but how routine and emotionless it appears" (tool's paraphrase of his claim). German source, translated by the tool. Source: [evangelisch.de](https://www.evangelisch.de/inhalte/99306/07-04-2010)
- Nelson Minar (blog, 2 March 2008): real gunship video "struck me how much this real war video was like a video game." He says the game's level uses the same visuals and basic setup and reuses some of the same dialog, and calls the effect "creepy." A blogger's claim, not checked against the footage. Source: [Somebits](https://www.somebits.com/weblog/culture/games/games-imitate-war-imitate-games.html)
- Wikipedia's CoD4 page does not cover the mission beyond one plot line ("they survive with support from an AC-130 gunship"). Source: [Wikipedia: CoD4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Call_of_Duty_4:_Modern_Warfare)

Spec Ops: The Line (white phosphorus)
- Cameron Koch (Game Informer, 16 April 2014): the player launches a drone, opens a "targeting laptop," and fires from behind the screen. Walker's reflected face is visible on the screen, "emotionless, even stoic." Afterward the player finds the smoking civilians, a mother and child embracing, and the squad shouts but "you can barely hear them." Koch reads the scene as a critique of shooters that hide the ground-level cost of aerial strikes. Source: [Game Informer](https://gameinformer.com/b/features/archive/2014/04/16/moments-white-phosphorus-in-spec-ops-the-line)
- Loading screens begin as gameplay hints and turn hostile, sometimes addressing the player ("To kill for yourself is murder"); the team made them "increasingly aggressive" to provoke players. Source: [Wikipedia: Spec Ops](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spec_Ops:_The_Line)

September 12th (Gonzalo Frasca, Newsgaming, 2003)
- Premise: the goal is to cause little collateral damage, because "too much destruction may cause other civilians to become terrorists." Source: [Wikipedia: Newsgame](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newsgame)
- Jill Walker Rettberg (2003 blog): after firing a rocket the player waits for reloading and hears mourning women; one mourner's clothing alternates between a civilian dress and a terrorist outfit until it settles on the terrorist's. Source: [jill/txt](https://jilltxt.net/newsgaming)
- Mark Nelson (2009) classes it as unwinnable, a "futile strategy" game "intended to highlight how bombing terrorists is a futile strategy, because it just produces more of them." Source: [Nelson](https://www.kmjn.org/notes/newsgame_rhetorics.html)

Ender's Game (novel)
- Ender believes he is running a final simulation; afterward Mazer Rackham reveals the "simulations" were "real battles, directing human spacecraft against bugger fleets via an ansible." Training vocabulary ("game," "simulator," "Battle Room") conceals real combat. Source: [Wikipedia: Ender's Game](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ender%27s_Game)
- Planted early: at Battle School Ender plays a simulated adventure game about being killed by, then killing, a giant; on the colony world he finds a structure that matches that game's simulation. Source: [Wikipedia: Ender's Game](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ender%27s_Game)

### Inferences
- In every case the player's inputs are the same before and after the reveal. Only the label, the camera, or the player's knowledge changes (Severance sorting, Ender's simulator, Unmanned's mission screen, Spec Ops' laptop).
- Euphemism sits in the system's voice (Lumon's names, the program called Orwell, the crew's chatter, Beholder's "Blissful sleep"), not in a villain's speech. Players absorb it as interface text.
- Distance is built from three dials: image (grey, fuzzy), time (delay, reload wait), sound (flat voice, muffled aftermath). Unmanned and Spec Ops turn all three. CoD4 turns the voice dial. September 12th turns the time dial.
- Distance ends in one of three ways: aftermath is shown (Spec Ops), a tally pays off (Papers, Please), or a label is decoded (Severance's tempers, Ender's "simulation", Undertale's LV).
- A satellite is already "at a distance" by premise, so these precedents bear most on the puzzle UI and its feedback.
- Mundane loops risk boredom. Albor defends it as deliberate for Unmanned, while a Papers, Please reviewer found the loop "tedious" (see section 6). Short batches limit the risk.

### Gaps
- No fetched source confirms the AC-130 mission's interface look (grey, thermal). Minar says only "same visuals." No developer statement on the mission was found.
- September 12th: the on-screen "not a game" text, view and cursor behavior were not retrieved (the Electronic Literature Organization page rendered empty to the fetch tool twice).
- Unmanned: no Wikipedia page at the guessed title. Designers' own words are limited to the festival blurb and a quoted self-description.
- Beholder: no designer statement of intent in fetched sources.
- Severance: no fetched source says what the refinement is "for." The Nerdist piece mixes confirmed show details with speculation.

## 2. Two perspectives on the same events: how does the second perspective recast the first?

### Takeaway
A second perspective recasts the first when the structure repeats, a few new facts change what earlier events mean, and the mechanics themselves can carry the shift (Brothers).

### Cited Findings
NieR: Automata
- First playthrough follows 2B, second follows 9S, third splits between A2 and 9S. The page lists 26 endings (five main, A to E; 21 game-over, F to Z). Source: [Wikipedia: NieR: Automata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nier:_Automata)
- In the 9S playthrough he "discovers a glitch in YoRHa's servers when syncing himself and 2B" and learns "humanity was extinct long before the alien invasion." The Machines are shown replicating human societies. Source: [Wikipedia: NieR: Automata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nier:_Automata)
- Heather Alexandra and Mike Fahey (Kotaku, 22 March 2017): "The second playthrough is mostly repeating the process of the first with a different character." The 9S route opens differently and reveals more about the Tower. Their argument, in the tool's paraphrase, is that the game gains meaning through repetition and each replay reframes earlier events. Source: [Kotaku](https://kotaku.com/every-time-nier-automata-ends-it-gets-better-1793524744)
- Yoko Taro wanted a "dry" narrative and more room for players to interpret the story. Trailers obscured 2B's fate by using A2's early-game model. Source: [Wikipedia: NieR: Automata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nier:_Automata)

Brothers: A Tale of Two Sons
- Two thumbsticks move the brothers individually and the triggers interact. The story is told through "actions, gestures and expressions," and all dialogue is "a fictional language based on Lebanese Arabic." Source: [Wikipedia: Brothers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brothers:_A_Tale_of_Two_Sons)
- Ian McCamant (Game Developer, 12 May 2020): the younger brother Naiee cannot swim and clings to Naia. After Naia dies, Naiee alone only wades waist-deep. The breakthrough is using Naia's stick as well, which launches a breaststroke. McCamant reads it as Naiee "using the memory of his brother." Source: [Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/brothers-all-the-feels-in-one-input)

The Last of Us Part II
- Control "intermittently switches between Ellie and Abby." Abby's thread opens "Three days earlier." A July 2025 update added a chronological mode "intertwining Ellie and Abby's stories." Source: [Wikipedia: TLOU2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Last_of_Us_Part_II)
- Neil Druckmann wanted players "to hate Abby early in the game, but later empathize with her," and to feel a "thirst for revenge" before realizing the reality of their actions. Source: [Wikipedia: TLOU2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Last_of_Us_Part_II)
- Game Informer preview (26 September 2019): human enemies "coordinate their attacks and even call to each other by name," and killing a dog made the writer "feel like a monster." The preview does not give Naughty Dog's rationale. Source: [Game Informer](https://www.gameinformer.com/preview/2019/09/26/the-four-biggest-takeaways-from-playing-last-of-us-part-ii)
- Critics: Polygon and Kotaku argued the game repeated its themes "without allowing the player any agency in their decisions." Kat Bailey criticized the gap between its anti-violence message and the combat it requires. Source: [Wikipedia: TLOU2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Last_of_Us_Part_II)

### Inferences
- A second perspective is cheapest when it reuses assets. NieR's second run "mostly repeats" the first, and TLOU2 overlaps timelines. For a game with two selves, replaying a space from the other self is the lowest-cost form.
- The recast comes from a few new facts in the replay (NieR: the server glitch, the Tower), not from new levels.
- Brothers shows a control change that carries an emotional beat. The planned glasses-break (slower, near-blind, pebbles) is already a mechanic change at a story beat; what it means is Michael's call.
- Names (TLOU2) and numbers (Severance, Papers, Please) are opposite label choices for the same job of making a target feel like a person or a unit.
- Risks: TLOU2 critics say empathy switching can repeat a message without giving agency. A control-based reveal like Brothers' may be missed by non-gamers who do not notice stick mapping.

### Gaps
- No direct designer statement on the swim mechanic (Fares, Starbreeze) found; the reading is McCamant's.
- Naughty Dog's reason for named enemies not found.
- Fetched pages use "playthroughs" and lettered endings. "Route B" is the brief's and fans' term, and no fetched source uses it. A designer interview (godisageek) did not cover the replay structure.

## 3. Reveals that recast the protagonist: how is foreshadowing planted so the reveal feels earned?

### Takeaway
The earned reveals plant the answer early in a form the player cannot yet read: a repeated phrase, an odd fade, a lying label, a world that already describes the protagonist. The designers who explain their method say to plant enough clues that the reveal feels prepared even to players who guess.

### Cited Findings
Braid
- In the final world time runs backward for everything except Tim. It "depicts the princess escaping from a knight," reversing the chase, and Tim is "revealed to be the 'monster' from whom the princess is running." Source: [Wikipedia: Braid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Braid_(video_game))
- Planted earlier: the premise says the princess "has been snatched by a horrible and evil monster," Tim "made a mistake which he hopes to reconcile or erase," and most world endings say "the princess is in another castle." Source: [Wikipedia: Braid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Braid_(video_game))
- Jonathan Blow: the game is "about the journey, not the destination" and he withheld the full plot until completion. Edge found the story "a little trite in its self-conscious obscurity" outside the final world. Source: [Wikipedia: Braid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Braid_(video_game))

BioShock
- "Would you kindly" "has preceded many of Atlas's commands as a hypnotic trigger." Jack reads a letter on the plane that contains the phrase, and the opening crash was added late partly to allude to the twist. When Ryan uses it, Jack's earlier guidance reads as coerced. Source: [Wikipedia: BioShock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BioShock)
- Debate: Clint Hocking coined "ludonarrative dissonance" in a 2007 critique of the game. Jonathan Blow argued the sisters' moral choice had little impact. A 2026 reassessment by Connor Jackson disputes Hocking. Source: [Wikipedia: BioShock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BioShock)

Spec Ops: The Line
- Cues that Walker is unreliable: déjà vu after the helicopter crash ("we did this already!"), hallucinations from the start (Williams: a billboard with Konrad's face), a flashback showing Konrad's radio "lacked a battery," and fades to white when Walker is hallucinating or deceiving himself. Source: [Wikipedia: Spec Ops](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spec_Ops:_The_Line)
- Walt Williams: the "best stories are the ones that have stuff hidden in them," events are "open to interpretation," and a valid ending is to "put the controller down and stop playing." Source: [Wikipedia: Spec Ops](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spec_Ops:_The_Line)
- Kirk Hamilton (Kotaku, 24 July 2012), on the civilian scene: Williams said "We wanted for you to feel very pressured and not know what to do," that "everything that could be controversial was absolutely necessary," and that a booby-trapped-civilian idea was cut as too contrived. Source: [Kotaku](https://kotaku.com/how-to-kill-civilians-in-a-war-game-5928765)

Hotline Miami
- Before each chapter the player picks an animal mask. Masked figures question Jacket; Richard asks "do you like hurting other people?" The figures foreshadow events (hinting at the murder of Jacket's girlfriend) but never reveal Jacket's identity. Richard says Jacket was reliving events while comatose. The Biker's epilogue traces the calls to a group called 50 Blessings. Source: [Wikipedia: Hotline Miami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hotline_Miami)
- G. Christopher Williams (PopMatters, 9 January 2013): the answering-machine goals are "coded" as ordinary tasks such as fixing plumbing, picking up a package or making a delivery, while the player is really sent to kill everyone at the location. The questioners wear the same masks the player wears, linking player and protagonist, and the question challenges the player. Source: [PopMatters](https://www.popmatters.com/167026-more-masks-more-deviants-understanding-our-role-in-hotline-miami-2495786915.html)
- Conflict between sources: the Wikipedia extract says each message tells him to go elsewhere and "kill all enemies at that location," and gives the first job as retrieving a briefcase. It does not frame the calls as disguised. The coded-errand reading is one critic's interpretation. Source: [Wikipedia: Hotline Miami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hotline_Miami); contradicted in emphasis by [PopMatters](https://www.popmatters.com/167026-more-masks-more-deviants-understanding-our-role-in-hotline-miami-2495786915.html)
- Developers called it an "arcade game first, and a reality simulator second." Pitchfork's Nina Corcoran argued the soundtrack creates anxiety while numbing the player to the violence. The central debate is whether it critiques violence or indulges it. Source: [Wikipedia: Hotline Miami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hotline_Miami)

Knights of the Old Republic
- James Ohlen and Drew Karpyshyn cited The Sixth Sense as a model for planting enough clues that the reveal feels prepared even to players who anticipate it. Karpyshyn wanted enough foreshadowing for the reveal to feel earned rather than arbitrary (tool paraphrase). Ohlen called Revan "essentially the player" and also cited Fight Club. Source: [Wikipedia: Revan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revan)
- The game first presents Revan as a dead Sith Lord whose crimes are repeatedly described, so the world describes the protagonist before the reveal. The Jedi Council erasing Revan's memory is the in-story concealment. Game Informer ranked a story twist number two in 2007. Source: [Wikipedia: Revan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revan); [Wikipedia: KOTOR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star_Wars:_Knights_of_the_Old_Republic)

Undertale
- Sans reveals that "LOVE" and "EXP" are acronyms for "level of violence" and "execution points," after Flowey "initially lies about LV." Killing earns EXP, which raises LOVE. Source: [Wikipedia: Undertale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Undertale)
- The game remembers: on later playthroughs "the dialogue will be altered depending on actions in previous playthroughs," and on the Genocide route "many characters evacuate to escape the player." Source: [Wikipedia: Undertale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Undertale)
- Toby Fox called not killing "the crux of one of the major themes." Sean Travers read the game as "a critique of the violent nature of mainstream role-playing video games." Source: [Wikipedia: Undertale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Undertale)

### Inferences
Patterns in how the clue is planted:
- Hide the trigger somewhere harmless and repeat it (BioShock's letter and commands).
- Give the answer in a form the player cannot read yet (Braid's opening premise, Spec Ops' déjà vu, Hotline Miami's masks).
- Let a trusted interface lie about a label (Undertale's Flowey on LV).
- Use presentation as a code (Spec Ops' white fades).
- Let the world describe the protagonist first (KOTOR's Revan).
- Budget the clues so the reveal is prepared even for players who guess (KOTOR designers, The Sixth Sense).
- The recast reuses an action the player already performed: BioShock's commands, Spec Ops' firing, Undertale's kills. That is why these reveals double as commentary on player complicity (see section 6).

### Gaps
- KOTOR's specific early clues (dreams, party reactions) were not in the fetched pages; only the designers' stated approach was.
- Braid's book text and world titles were not confirmed in the fetched page.
- Hotline Miami's exact message wording was not retrieved.

## 4. Meta and UI-based reveals (Inscryption, Doki Doki Literature Club, Pony Island)

### Takeaway
These reveals break the container (game window, operating system, files) and use glitches, fake errors and warnings that grow over time. A browser game for non-gamers can keep the effect inside the canvas, as a DDLC release did.

### Cited Findings
Inscryption
- Act I: the player sits in Leshy's cabin "unaware they are playing a game within a game," and cabin puzzles hint at the card forms of P03, Grimora and Magnificus. Act II reframes Act I as a Pokemon-like top-down RPG, the "original" game. Act III recasts earlier acts as P03's ploy to access the player-character's computer, and Act I's death cards return as opponents. Source: [Wikipedia: Inscryption](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inscryption)
- Meta layer: live-action found-footage videos; a floppy disk offering only "Continue Game"; the character Carder's camera malfunctions escalating from the start; P03 using Carder's internet connection and files; characters warning him not to open "OLD_DATA"; an ARG with floppy disks mailed to fans. Source: [Wikipedia: Inscryption](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inscryption)
- Daniel Mullins started it as a Ludum Dare 43 entry (theme "sacrifices must be made") and later added full-motion video. Source: [Wikipedia: Inscryption](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inscryption)

Doki Doki Literature Club!
- It "initially appears to be a light-hearted dating simulator." After the first playthrough anomalies build up: "illegible text," "distorted character sprites," and other glitches "become commonplace." Monika deletes characters' files and the player must manipulate game files to advance. The Plus! version "replaces this with a 'virtual desktop.'" Source: [Wikipedia: DDLC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doki_Doki_Literature_Club!)
- Planted early: Monika is left out of the poem minigame "for narrative reasons"; Sayori's poem "get out of my head" foreshadows her death; "light, bouncy, gentle and playful" music encourages attachment, which makes the breakdown hit harder. Source: [Wikipedia: DDLC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doki_Doki_Literature_Club!)
- Dan Salvato wanted things "scary because they make you uncomfortable, not because they shove scary-looking things in your face" and built a cute facade that breaks down. Source: [Wikipedia: DDLC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doki_Doki_Literature_Club!)
- The official page carries a content warning: "This game is not suitable for children or those who are easily disturbed." Source: [DDLC official site](https://ddlc.moe)

Pony Island
- Presented "as a crude arcade game," mainly point-and-click, with "user-interface metaphors, such as a simulated operating system." The player reorders commands in pseudocode to bypass "corruptions," and the game is "corrupted by Lucifer himself." Source: [Wikipedia: Pony Island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pony_Island)
- Mullins built "fake error screens and messages through the Steam software that appeared to come from the player's friends," and wanted a game that "felt like it was not meant to be played." Source: [Wikipedia: Pony Island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pony_Island)
- IGN (9.0) called it "a punk rock experiment in storytelling and game design that delights in toying with the player." Source: [Wikipedia: Pony Island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pony_Island)

### Inferences
- The safe subset for a browser game shown to non-gamers: corrupted text and sprites, fake error dialogs, and a prompt that offers a replay, all drawn inside the game canvas. No file or OS tricks. DDLC Plus! swapping file edits for a virtual desktop is a documented precedent for keeping the effect inside the app.
- Glitches work better when they grow (DDLC) and when a character warns the player (Inscryption's OLD_DATA).
- Risk: a non-gamer audience may read glitches as real bugs. A consistent, rare visual vocabulary would help.
- The planned "dots that suddenly and randomly move" routing variant resembles Unmanned's drifting tracker and could carry either a neutral or a glitch reading.

### Gaps
- Inscryption's desktop and file tricks were not confirmed on the fetched page (the extract did not describe them).
- No hands-on play of any title. Judging how the glitches look needs screenshots or clips (see Needs Michael or Chrome).

## 5. Technique list: euphemistic labels, numbers that stand for people, glitches, environmental details, motifs, the second look

### Takeaway
Ten techniques recur. The cheapest to build are text and audio techniques (labels, tally, motif), then fades and glitches. Replay modes cost more, and control changes depend on level design (build costs here are my inferences).

### Cited Findings

## 6. Framing for a class audience: how serious games handle violence, terrorism and remote killing

### Takeaway
The reported debate runs on a few axes: warn or skip versus force the experience, show aftermath versus withhold it, moralize versus stay ambiguous, and whether boredom is a flaw or the point. This section reports the debate and makes no recommendation.

### Cited Findings
Warnings and skip options
- No Russian (Modern Warfare 2): a pre-mission warning lets players skip the level with no loss of achievements or progress. Designer Mohammad Alavi said the skip was added so players would not be punished for refusing to do what they felt was morally wrong. The Guardian's Keith Stuart called the skip a "cop-out." Lead writer Jesse Stern said the aim was to "put you as close as possible to atrocity." Source: [Wikipedia: No Russian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/No_Russian)
- No Russian reception and regional handling: Rock Paper Shotgun's Kieron Gillen called it "dumb shock"; PC World's Matt Peckham criticized "plausible emotional deniability"; Destructoid's Sterling said it showed games could tackle controversial topics. Activision removed the level from Russian versions; Japanese and German versions gave a game over for killing civilians. Source: [Wikipedia: No Russian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/No_Russian)
- DDLC's official page carries a content warning ("not suitable for children or those who are easily disturbed"). Wikipedia records continuing disputes about its rating and platform availability despite it. Source: [DDLC official site](https://ddlc.moe); [Wikipedia: DDLC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doki_Doki_Literature_Club!)

Justification and necessity
- Walt Williams: "everything that could be controversial was absolutely necessary." Kirk Hamilton judged the Spec Ops civilian scene "appropriate and purposeful" in contrast to No Russian. Source: [Kotaku](https://kotaku.com/how-to-kill-civilians-in-a-war-game-5928765)
- Spec Ops reception: praised by IGN's Mitch Dyer and Wired's Curtis Silver; criticized for "unsatisfying, hollow, and binary" choices (Eurogamer, Game Informer), "tonal dissonance" between gameplay and story, and an escapist-fantasy combat loop. Some called the massacre unnecessary or exploitative, and Williams said it was meant to provoke anger. Source: [Wikipedia: Spec Ops](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spec_Ops:_The_Line)

Aftermath, abstraction and remote killing
- Spec Ops shows the aftermath of an aerial strike. CoD4's AC-130 mission is described by commentators as flat and routine, and Terbuyken says the real scandal in the Collateral Murder video "is not the shooting itself but how routine and emotionless it appears" (tool paraphrase). He also reports Washington Post coverage that the crew followed rules of engagement. Source: [Game Informer](https://gameinformer.com/b/features/archive/2014/04/16/moments-white-phosphorus-in-spec-ops-the-line); [evangelisch.de](https://www.evangelisch.de/inhalte/99306/07-04-2010)
- Unmanned: Albor reads the boredom as a formal choice and cites Greg Costikyan agreeing it is boring on purpose. Rauch calls it "darkly ironic" but "nuanced." Source: [Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/unmanned-and-the-rhetoric-of-division); [Video Games for the Arts](https://www.videogamesforthearts.com/articles/unmanned)
- Papers, Please: Pope aimed for a non-judgmental narrative and said he did not set out to make an "empathy game." Reviewer Stephanie Bendixsen found it "tedious," while it won IGF's Seumas McNally Grand Prize and a 2021 Peabody Award. Source: [Wikipedia: Papers, Please](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Papers,_Please)

Moralizing versus ambiguity
- Orwell: the designers said they wanted players to "make up their own minds" and admitted "we have in fact frustrated many players." Game Informer wrote it is "simply content to repeat what novels and films have more eloquently." Source: [Game Developer interview](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/road-to-the-igf-osmotic-studios-i-orwell-i-); [Wikipedia: Orwell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orwell_(video_game))
- September 12th: Frasca's FAQ answers "Are your games biased?" with "Of course they are." Jesper Juul (2003) said it "was clearly made with the intent of expressing an opinion," faulted its "cartoonish clearness," and wanted games to show doubt and ambiguity. Source: [jill/txt](https://jilltxt.net/newsgaming); [Juul](https://www.jesperjuul.net/ludologist/2003/09/30/ideological-games-september-12th/)
- Hotline Miami: the debate is whether it critiques violence or indulges it (Pitchfork's Corcoran, Kunzelman, Eurogamer's Tapsell). Source: [Wikipedia: Hotline Miami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hotline_Miami)
- TLOU2: critics said its themes repeat without player agency, and Kat Bailey flagged the gap between its anti-violence message and required combat. Source: [Wikipedia: TLOU2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Last_of_Us_Part_II)
- Ender's Game: John Kessel says the twist lets Ender "remain morally clean," while Mike Ryder says Ender "is exploited by powers beyond his control." Source: [Wikipedia: Ender's Game](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ender%27s_Game)

### Inferences
- The same four questions recur across the works: is there a warning or a skip, is the aftermath shown, does the work tell the player what to think, and is the dullness intended. A class audience will likely ask them of any such game.
- Several designers (Pope, Osmotic, Frasca) state their stance on bias or judgment on the record, which gives a ready citation style for a designer's note. This is a description of what exists, not a recommendation.

- No academic game-studies papers or GDC talks were retrieved. The debate is from Wikipedia reception summaries, critics' essays and designer interviews.
- No source found on how non-gamer audiences respond to these devices.
