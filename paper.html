Unseen AboveSpace Law and the Power to See and Not Be Seen

TL;DR

TL;DR

Space law could work for people if States chose it to. Powerful States instead choose to use space law and space to protect themselves from being seen, rather than protecting people from being seen. In 2026, the US government “asked” satellite companies to obscure imagery of its war with Iran: the public was denied seeing while the US government retained its own view, and the people being imaged, as every day, were never asked. Article VI of the Outer Space Treaty obliges States to supervise space activities by their nationals, including companies, and that could apply to people, too.

SUMMARY

Executive summary

In April 2026, the U.S. government "requested" that satellite companies withhold imagery of the war with Iran. The public lost its view; the government kept its own. This paper argues that the pattern is built into the law.

  • Part I. Space law's data rules were written for the states that do the sensing. The treaties count objects, not pictures; the 1986 Remote Sensing Principles refused sensed states a veto; national licensing screens data for national security, never for the people in the frame.
  • Part II. One state got a right not to be seen clearly: the Kyl-Bingaman Amendment caps U.S. imagery of Israel, and in practice the blur covers Palestine too. NOAA calls privacy "outside the scope of the Act." The statute's "international obligations" clause says otherwise.
  • Part III. The quieter lever is the checkbook. The formal "shutter control" tool has never been used; contracts and "voluntary" requests did the work in Afghanistan, Ukraine, Gaza and Iran, off the record and outside the guardrails, with real First Amendment and Article 19 problems.
  • Part IV. The people being watched get almost nothing: RF sensing is unlicensed, satellite internet makes users findable, and the Fourth Amendment offers thin protection at home and none abroad.
  • Part V. Article VI, read with Article III and the ICCPR, already requires states to supervise orbital data for people: privacy criteria in licensing, withholds only on the record, licensing for RF sensing, and public reporting of government requests. No new treaty is needed.
INTRO

Introduction

Planet emailed subscribers on April 4, 2026, about five weeks into a US-Israeli bombing campaign targeting Iran. "Due to the conflict in the Middle East," it read, "the U.S. government has requested all satellite imagery providers voluntarily implement an indefinite withhold of imagery in the designated Area of Interest."1 A month earlier, the same company had insisted that its first delays were "not the result of a directive or requirement from any government."2 There was now a sender to that request.

Notice, too, who did not go dark. According to a Planet spokesperson, the U.S. government, "as well as some foreign governments and commercial users," still had unrestricted access to the war imagery.3 The public had no view of the war. The government kept its own.

And yet, those same satellites continue to take pictures of ordinary people every single day. Those people never got an email, and nothing in space law gives them a say.

This paper claims that this is no accident. When space law determines what a satellite can see, it shields the powerful from being seen, not people from being watched. Donna Haraway had a name for the privilege at stake: "the power to see and not be seen."4 These powerful states hold both halves of that power in their hands, and they can even grant the second half to their allies. Most sensed states, and the people below them, don't have either. But that's not a gap, that's a decision because the tool to close it is in the treaty.

This paper proceeds as follows. Part I shows that space law's data rules were created with the sensing states in mind. Part II will examine the only place in which U.S. law recognizes a right not to be seen. Part III follows the quieter lever: contracts and "requests." Part IV asks what the law provides those being monitored (very little). Part V argues that a closer reading of the Outer Space Treaty's Article VI, focused on what it actually says, requires states to supervise orbital data for people and not just for themselves.

PART I

Space Law's Data Rules Are Written for Sensing States

But if a satellite takes a picture of you, whose problem is that under space law? A state's, and usually only the camera-owning state gets a real say.

A. The Treaties Count Objects, Not Pictures

To kick off, the Liability Convention defines "damage" as "loss of life, personal injury or other impairment of health; or loss of or damage to property."5 Those are a few of the things a falling object can do. A camera does none of that. Von der Dunk reports the majority view that damage "caused by an intrusion of privacy" through satellite data "should not be held compensable under the Liability Convention."6 And the regime knows who pays when a satellite lands on a barn. It says nothing about what the satellite actually saw during its descent.

B. The Data Rules That Exist Protect States, Thinly

The closest thing to a data code in space law is the 1986 Remote Sensing Principles. They record a defeat. In COPUOS, the Soviets, the French and much of the developing world sought the prior consent of the imaged state for dissemination of the imagery; the U.S. and its allies favored unrestricted dissemination.7 Consent lost.8 The sensed state got consolation prizes: sensing must not harm "the legitimate rights and interests of the sensed State," and the sensed state may buy data on its territory "on a non-discriminatory basis and on reasonable cost terms."9 As von der Dunk puts it, the sensed state "has no veto to prevent it from being 'sensed.'"10

Notice whose rights these are. The Principles say nothing about individuals or human rights, and "[p]rivacy aspects of VHR remote sensing data dissemination, at least, have not been considered."11 Even state privacy fares poorly: Lyall and Larsen weigh the claim that sensing infringes "the privacy of a state" and find "a more cogent argument" for monitoring.12 We can borrow Stefoudi's term for what remains: sovereign privacy, a privacy that belongs to governments, not people.13

The remainder of the toolkit is constructed in a similar fashion. The ITU Constitution ensures the confidentiality of international correspondence and then authorizes member states to surrender it to their own authorities.14 The rare exception, the 1982 Direct Broadcasting Principles' right "to seek, receive and impart information," passed by vote over U.S. opposition.15

And none of it was by accident. Washington, worried that ICAO involvement would "civilianise" space and threaten plans for a spy satellite programme, persuaded ICAO's new president to keep space off the agenda.16

C. National Licensing Screens Data for the State

Domestic law finishes the job. A U.S. license issues only if the applicant will honor "any applicable international obligations and national security concerns of the United States."17 Other licensing states ask the same question:

  • Canada may interrupt service to protect "national security, defence, foreign policy interests and/or upholding Canada's international obligations."18
  • Japan asks whether use "is likely to cause adverse effect on ensuring the peace and security of the international community and the national security of Japan."19
  • France protects "defense and foreign policy and compliance with international obligations."20

Harris and Baumann find these regimes "mainly driven by national security concerns."21 Nobody's got a privacy test for the people in the frame.

A sensed state can buy photos of itself, but it can't stop photos from being taken. With one exception. Since 1996, one state has possessed a right that the others don't: a statutory right not to be clearly perceived. Part II turns to it.

PART II

Formal Control: A Right Not to Be Seen, for One State

Under the 1986 Principles, a state can take images of another state's territory without requesting permission, and the state being imaged only gets a chance to access the information.22 Then U.S. law did a strange thing: it gave that right, in slightly altered form, to only one state.

A. One sentence, one state

The Kyl-Bingaman Amendment accomplishes that purpose in a single sentence. A U.S. agency "may issue a license for the collection or dissemination by a non-Federal entity of satellite imagery with respect to Israel" "only if such imagery is no more detailed or precise than satellite imagery of Israel that is available from commercial sources."23 It sets no number and defines no territory. It pegs Israel's protection to the market: American companies may show Israel no more sharply than a foreign seller already does.

For more than twenty years regulators read that as 2 meters. Then, in July 2020, NOAA found that imagery of Israel was "readily and consistently available from non-U.S. commercial sources at a resolution of 0.4 meters," and so it "changed the existing resolution limit of 2.0 m GSD to 0.4 m GSD."24 Amnon Harari, then head of space programs at Israel's Defense Ministry, told Kan public radio: "We would always prefer to be photographed at the lowest resolution possible. It's always preferable to be seen blurred, rather than precisely."25

We can call this a right to be blurred: not a right to go unseen, but a statutory right to be seen less clearly than the technology allows. International law didn't give any sensed state that much. Under US law, it went to one.

B. Where does "Israel" end?

Consider a question the statute never answers: does "Israel" include the West Bank, Gaza, or the Golan Heights? NOAA has never said. The 2020 notice speaks only of "Israel" and the "State of Israel," and defines neither.26 If you read section 1064 literally, it could allow imaging Palestine at full resolution. "Yet, in practice, this is not the case."27 As Zena Agha put it, "in practice, the KBA's restrictions apply throughout Palestine-Israel."28 Michael Fradley describes the limit as reaching the occupied territories and the Golan "by implicit extension."29 Firms round up: "if a corner of the image includes Israel, we treat that image as if KBA applies to the whole scene."30

Of course, the extension was not uniform. Zerbini and Fradley found West Bank and Golan tiles that had not been degraded, and they described "multiple and often contradicting interpretations by U.S. regulators."31 And yet the unevenness is the whole point: the scope of the blur was established by practice, by regulators who didn't push and companies who didn't risk. This is already informal control, which Part III addresses directly.

C. Who pays, and who gets protected

But, who pays for the blur? Palestinians taking photos of demolitions, settlements, and war damage, along with the investigators, archaeologists, and journalists who have worked alongside them. Agha argues the law "is better characterized as censorship," since it "hinders the work of archaeologists, environmentalists, geographers, and humanitarians."32 James Walker, who interviewed human rights analysts, agrees the limit "does block some forms of VHR dependent analysis."33

Compare this with how that same regime acts toward people. The law mandates that all licenses make a sensed government's territorial data available to the government.34 In its 2020 rule, NOAA kept that condition, now an offer made "[u]pon request," over a privacy objection, because "[t]he Act requires Commerce to include this condition, so Commerce cannot lawfully omit this condition."35 When commenters asked NOAA to address privacy itself, the answer was short: "such concerns are outside the scope of the Act. These requests are better addressed to Congress."36 To put it another way, the law protects sensed governments, and the agency says it can do nothing for sensed people.

But is that right? The statute has an opening. No license may issue unless the applicant will comply with "any applicable international obligations . . . of the United States."37 Licensees must also operate "to observe the international obligations of the United States."38 The United States is party to the ICCPR, which bars arbitrary interference with privacy and protects the freedom to seek and receive information.39 Of course, there are limits. The Covenant is not self-executing.40 And § 60122(b)(1) ties its obligations clause to § 60146, the FCC's radio frequency section.41 However, neither of these limits mandates the NOAA interpretation. "Outside the scope" was a choice about what "international obligations" means.

PART III

Informal Control: Shutter Control by Checkbook

On March 11, 2026, Planet Labs told the press that its new hold on Middle East imagery "is not the result of a directive or requirement from any government. It is Planet's decision."42 Less than four weeks later, Planet's notice to customers told a different story: "the U.S. government has requested all satellite imagery providers voluntarily implement an indefinite withhold of imagery in the designated Area of Interest."43 So which was it? We can't tell. That is the problem. In recent conflicts, private gatekeeping and government "requests," not the formal legal process, decided what the public could see from orbit.

A. The Tool Nobody Uses

There's actually a tool for this on paper. NOAA's rules require higher-tier licensees to obey "limited-operations directives issued by the Secretary" that "temporarily limit data collection and/or dissemination during periods of increased concerns for national security and where necessary to meet international obligation or foreign policy interests."44 The trade calls this "shutter control," and it has thin but real guardrails. The Secretary of Defense or State must make the call, and that function "shall not be delegated below the Secretary or acting Secretary."45 Conditions "should be imposed for the smallest area and for the shortest period necessary."46 Of course, the agreement also says it creates "[n]o legal rights or remedies."47

It has never been used: "[t]he government has never formally invoked that authority, though it has reached in that direction."48 Why reach, rather than invoke?

B. The Contract Is the Lever

Buying is more convenient than ordering. In October 2001, just as the bombing campaign against Afghanistan commenced, the government's imagery agency purchased exclusive imagery rights from Space Imaging's Ikonos, which was the only commercial satellite capable of taking high-resolution images of Afghanistan.49

The checkbook has only grown. In 2022, the National Reconnaissance Office gave BlackSky, Maxar and Planet decade-long contracts worth billions.50 Maxar's alone was worth up to $3.24 billion.51 The military depends on these firms,52 but they depend on it more. Consider Ukraine. In March 2025, an NGA spokesperson said that, "[i]n accordance with the Administration's directive on support to Ukraine, NGA has temporarily suspended access to GEGD," its portal for government-bought commercial imagery.53 A whole country's view went dark by account setting, not legal act.

C. Gaza and Iran

Gaza, 2023. NOAA said it was "not considering additional restrictions," and Maxar said it was the "primary decision maker" on whether and when to release an image.54 Planet told NPR it "decided to apply additional review for SkySat imagery over the conflict region."55 The companies, not the law, decided.

Iran, 2026. Planet's holds escalated from a 96-hour delay to the April 4 "indefinite withhold," retroactive to March 9.56 Not everyone went blind. Bloomberg reported, citing a Planet spokesperson, that "the US government, as well as some foreign governments and commercial users, still had unrestricted access to war imagery."57 The buyer kept the power to see and not be seen. The public lost it.

Vantor's story is murkier. In March, it said it "may implement enhanced access controls" but that these decisions were "not mandated by any government."58 It later told Bloomberg it hadn't received any requests.59 Yet the Washington Post reported in May that Vantor and Planet "have complied with requests from the U.S. government to limit, delay, or indefinitely withhold" publication.60 But neither I nor the public can resolve this contradiction. That is the point.

Let us call this pattern shutter control by checkbook. A wire reporter called the 2001 deal "checkbook shutter control,"61 and Witjes and Olbrich describe the government as the industry's biggest customer and its regulator.62

D. Why the Route Matters

To be sure, protecting "allied and NATO-partner personnel" may justify some limits.63 But international law permits only limits "provided by law and are necessary."64 The issue isn't about the limits per se. It is to limits made off the record.

First, the checkbook bypasses all of the safeguards built into the formal tool: there's no Secretary-level approval, no geographic or temporal constraints, no documentation. Moreover, it dodges an old prior restraint fight. Critics warned that "[i]f shutter control is exercised in any but the most compelling circumstances, a court challenge is inevitable."65 The request will not trigger any such challenge.

But even that informal pressure has limits under the First Amendment. "[C]oercion, persuasion, and intimidation" can amount to censorship, and compliance under them "was not voluntary."66 Prior restraints bear "a heavy presumption against [their] constitutional validity."67 Under NRA v. Vullo, the question is whether conduct "could be reasonably understood to convey a threat of adverse government action in order to punish or suppress" speech.68

More importantly, the remand renders the argument tighter. The Second Circuit granted qualified immunity because no case had clearly established that pressuring "third-party regulated entities into refraining from nonexpressive activity" was coercion.69 A company that refuses to publish its own images is not a third party. It is the speaker, because "the creation and dissemination of information are speech."70 A "request" from its licensor and biggest customer thus looks closer to direct coercion of a speaker than Vullo's facts did. That's an argument, not a holding. Standing is also a hurdle: Murthy demands "a substantial risk" of future restriction.71 It's not going to sue its biggest customer.

Finally, this is Article VI "continuing supervision" in practice.72 The McGill Manual agrees: "[a]uthorisation and continuing supervision by the appropriate State is required for all space activities of non-governmental entities, including those in support of military space activities."73 Nor, as the Dark and Quiet Skies II report puts it, can a state "circumvent a prohibition placed upon it by contracting its violative activity to a nongovernmental actor."74 In short, whenever a state is concerned about its own interest, supervision becomes more rapid and finer grained. It's done off the record.

What about those below us, those who do not wish to be seen? Part IV turns to them.

PART IV

The Exposed: What the Law Offers the People Being Watched

This Part asks what the law gives the people below. In short, space law gives them nothing; domestic law, just a bit.

A. Found, Not Photographed

Consider HawkEye 360. Their satellites don't take pictures, they detect radios. The company told the SEC that the National Reconnaissance Office "is our largest customer" and that 61% of its 2025 revenue came from U.S. government work.75 It also told investors that its "in-orbit RF sensing activities are not currently regulated by any single U.S. regulatory agency."76 That is just a correct interpretation of the rules. NOAA licenses only data that "can be processed into imagery of surface features of the Earth."77 NOAA advised HawkEye 360 in 2016 that it did not require a license.78 The FCC does not grant collection licenses, but only spectrum licenses.79 Buyers noticed. In 2019, Frontex spent roughly €1.5 million on a HawkEye 360 pilot that aimed to detect radio emitters out at sea.80

And satellite internet works the same way, in reverse. The terminal is a radio, and the network needs to know where it is. India's May 5, 2025 conditions require records of "UT location with precise latitude / longitude" and demand real-time tracking of terminals.81 The Ukrainian whitelist of 2026 (according to Cabinet Resolution No. 115) matches each civilian terminal with a national ID and a tax ID.82 And Starlink's January 15, 2026 privacy policy lets user data train AI models; the opt-out shown covers only "third-party collaborators."83 We can call this location by subscription: being online now means being findable.84

Imagery is no better. Even Sergio Marchisio, chairing COPUOS's Legal Subcommittee, conceded that the Remote Sensing Principles do not "provide an adequate discipline as regards the production, use and treatment of highly sophisticated and detailed imagery," including its implications for "individual privacy."85

B. What Domestic Law Offers

The Fourth Amendment affords Americans some protection, less than it appears. Consider the cases in turn:

  1. Dow Chemical. The Court said satellite surveillance "might be constitutionally proscribed absent a warrant," but only "as the Government concedes," and only in dictum.86
  2. Kyllo. Using a device "not in general public use" to look into a home is a search.87 But as Justice Stevens warned in dissent, the threat "will grow, rather than recede, as the use of intrusive equipment becomes more readily available."88
  3. Carpenter and Chatrie. Location records are protected, "even though for only a limited time, and from a third-party tech company."89 But Carpenter calls itself "a narrow one" and sets aside "collection techniques involving foreign affairs or national security."90 And Chatrie does not decide whether the government may simply buy the data.

Then there is everyone else. "The people" protected are those "part of a national community" or with "sufficient connection" to it.91

C. Who Is Most Exposed

Space law funnels all claims through states. But as Bin Cheng observed, absent damage, other states "may not even take the trouble of raising the issue, unless they see some national interests in doing so."92 HawkEye 360 made the same point from the other side: there have been "no formal concerns raised by Parties to the Treaty."93 So the most exposed are people without a powerful state to press their claims: migrants, the stateless, the occupied, and citizens of small states. Liberia's President Tubman put it plainly in 1966: "we share the same heavens with the greatest powers. Their catastrophes are usually ours."94

Is this a gap, then, or a choice? In Part V I argue that it is a choice.

PART V

Reading Article VI for People

Data is already covered by Article VI of the Outer Space Treaty, which, read in conjunction with Article III, places a requirement on States to supervise their operators to protect people, not just themselves.

A. Three Steps

Step one: supervision reaches data. Article VI provides that the activities of non-governmental entities in outer space "shall require authorization and continuing supervision by the appropriate State Party."95 But does "activities in outer space" include what happens to the data once it lands? Bin Cheng asked a version of that question about activities, such as the terms on which data are distributed, that take place on the ground but have their effects on Earth. His answer: "the answer appears to be in the affirmative."96 The Remote Sensing Principles point the same way. They define "remote sensing activities" to include "processing, interpreting and disseminating the processed data,"97 and Principle XIV puts state responsibility for those activities "[i]n compliance with article VI."98 And practice confirms it. Parts II and III show that oversight of orbital data is routine: limits on resolution, limited operations orders, sensed-government access. The question isn't about whether supervision reaches the data. It is whom supervision serves.

Step two: supervision must follow international law. Article III requires states to carry on space activities "in accordance with international law, including the Charter of the United Nations."99 The Charter, in turn, commits the United Nations to promote "universal respect for, and observance of, human rights," and every member "pledge[s]" to act toward that end.100 Under Article VI of the Treaty, the state is responsible for ensuring that national activities comply with the Treaty, including Article III.101 Cristian van Eijk puts the point well: "Space is pay-to-play, but human rights are not."102

Step three: the obligations. Which international law? Article 17 of the ICCPR bars "arbitrary or unlawful interference" with privacy and gives "[e]veryone . . . the right to the protection of the law against such interference."103 The Human Rights Committee reads that right as running against interference "whether [it] emanate[s] from State authorities or from natural or legal persons."104 It also says that "[t]he gathering and holding of personal information . . . whether by public authorities or private individuals or bodies, must be regulated by law."105 Article 19, meanwhile, protects the freedom "to seek, receive and impart information . . . regardless of frontiers."106 An imaging or RF-sensing satellite engages both rights at once.

B. What Article VI Adds

This is what's special about space. Under Article VI, as Cheng read it, a private operator's activities are "imputable to the State as if it were its own act."107 That matters. Ordinarily, a state's duty toward private actors is a positive one: to regulate and exercise due diligence. But if a licensee's collection counts as the state's own act, it engages the state's negative duty to respect privacy. Milanovic has argued that this duty to respect, unlike the duty to ensure, should be "territorially unlimited."108 Or, in other words, Article VI makes corporate data usage a state act.

This also answers Frans von der Dunk, who calls privacy in remote sensing "very much a national matter," something states may address through licensing if they choose.109 But once Steps one through three are in place, "may" becomes "must," at least for people within the licensing state's jurisdiction.

C. What Is New Here

To be sure, others have tied space law to privacy. Saboorian proposes new international data protection standards for satellite communications operators, built by blending the GDPR with the space treaties.110 Ferreira-Snyman wonders how human rights can be applied to humans who live in space.111 Freeland and Ireland-Piper, and Macchi, focus on corporate accountability, and both look mainly to new bodies or new due diligence laws.112 The closest work examines whether data centers in orbit are subject to Article VI oversight and whether privacy laws apply to data processed in orbit.113 This paper differs from these in three ways: it is based on the law as it stands, not as it might be proposed; it addresses imagery and RF sensing; and it protects people on Earth.

D. Objections and Responses

Objection one: the Principles do not cover this. They are limited to "improving natural resources management, land use and the protection of the environment,"114 and Lyall and Larsen say that reading in military sensing "would strain credulity."115 Response: Fair. Which is why step one is based on Cheng's interpretation of Article VI, with the Principles just serving as backup. Cheng's reading is contested. Stotler notes that Schmeichel's rival reading appears to follow the Vienna Convention more closely, and he suggests states may prefer it as militaries lean on commercial satellites.116 But Stotler also concedes that Cheng's view "has been roundly endorsed" and that "Cheng's interpretation has won the day."117 And his own thesis cuts my way: if interpretation is creation, then choosing to read supervision as protecting only states is a choice, too.

Objection two: privacy limits are limits on information. Response: Yes, and that is why one test governs both. Article 19 allows restrictions only "as are provided by law and are necessary," including "[f]or respect of the rights or reputations of others."118 Those privacy rules that pass that test are legal. Informal withholds by phone call, as in Part III, are not.

Objection three: the United States reads the ICCPR as territorial. It does, and the Committee "regrets" that position.119 The Committee's own test reaches "anyone within the power or effective control" of a state.120 Response: The claim holds, at a minimum, for people on U.S. territory. Beyond that, the gap is part of the finding: the people abroad who are most watched are the ones the sensing state says it owes nothing.

Objection four: the ICCPR is not self-executing. The 1992 declaration says so.121 Montgomery does the same for Article VI.122 Response: Goehring answers this directly. A non-self-executing treaty is still binding; it is simply "'addressed to' the political branches rather than the courts."123 Thus, the obligation lies with Congress and NOAA. And NOAA already has the hook: no license issues unless the Secretary finds in writing that the applicant will comply with "any applicable international obligations."124 Privacy is not "outside the scope of the Act."125 It is in the statute's own words.

Objection five: states spy on each other all the time. Response: They do, and international law largely tolerates it.126 That is a rule between nations. You don't commit espionage by licensing out a company to market pictures of individuals. It is trade, regulated by the state.

E. What Supervision for People Would Require

If this reading is right, Article VI requires at least four things:

  1. Privacy and public-access criteria in licensing, alongside the national security criteria already there.
  2. Withholds only through the formal process, on the record, so they can be tested under Article 19(3).
  3. Authorization for RF sensing, which, as one operator admits, is "not currently regulated by any single U.S. regulatory agency."127
  4. Public reporting of government requests to operators, so the supervisor is also supervised.

This would not need any new treaty. There needs to be a state that reads the old one for people.

A Note on the Companion Game

This paper has a companion: Unseen Above, a short pixel-art browser game.128 The player can alternate between these two roles. In one, you are a girl on a dying Earth, sneaking through a decaying mansion past cameras, sensors and a laser that seems to come from the side rather than from above. In the other, you're a satellite back end, where you solve neat routing and sorting problems. You can guess the turn: the puzzles were aiming the strikes at her, and the whole thing is a TV show watched by wealthy people on the Moon, who stay unseen until the end.

Why a game? Because the argument is about positions, and a game can put you in both. The player is both the watcher and the watched. The paperwork-like puzzles are Part III's contracts and "requests." The sourceless laser is informal control that leaves no record. The glasses that reveal the sensors, then crack, are the public losing its view of the powerful while staying visible to them. And the viewers on the Moon are the thesis itself.

Ingrid LaFleur describes Afrofuturism as a way to "craft decolonized destinies" through "speculative modalities such as science fiction, surrealism, magical realism, and horror."129 She also warns that satellites are crowding out the North Star that guided Harriet Tubman: "We will soon no longer be able to map our liberation in the skies."130 The games allow players to experience justice from within rather than simply to read about it.131

END

Conclusion

Space law already regulates the relationship between those who watch and those who are watched. Strong nations possess both powers and loan the latter to friendly countries. But most of the sensed states and those below them don't. The formal rules (Part I), the single exception written into law (Part II), the informal agreements (Part III), and the minimal safeguards for individuals (Part IV) all say the same thing.

And this, of course, is not permanent. Article VI already requires states to supervise what their companies do in orbit, and states already do it, constantly, whenever their own interests are on the line (Part V). The question is: who is that supervision for? Susan Marks, quoted by Ntina Tzouvala, describes a familiar pattern in international law: "Those who benefit from current arrangements remain comfortably out of view."132 But in orbit, that is not a metaphor. It is a licensing condition.

And the stakes are growing. Satellites have already started beaming communications from space, look at the Starlink constellation, and companies are now pitching the idea of putting data centers in orbit.133 That's the allocation that would follow the data up there, unless the oversight changes. The people beneath will be far more exposed than ever before, but the onlookers will stay concealed.
